# 24. Hire Systems, Not Skill Lists

Most job descriptions are inventories.

They list languages, platforms, years of experience, degrees, certifications, industries, frameworks, and interpersonal qualities. The inventory helps retrieval, but it often fails to describe the system the company needs the person to operate.

In the post-LLM economy, this failure becomes expensive.

A candidate may lack one named framework while possessing the architecture, learning system, and evidence discipline necessary to solve the problem. Another candidate may match every keyword while remaining unable to integrate models, tools, policy, evaluation, and organizational authority into a working outcome.

Companies should hire for systems.

## Begin with the outcome topology

A role specification should answer:

- What outcome must change?
- Which observations determine the work?
- Which decisions are repeated?
- Which systems must be connected?
- Which actions carry consequence?
- Which authority belongs to the role?
- Which evidence will establish success?
- What should become reusable?

This creates a topology rather than a shopping list.

For example, instead of:

> Ten years of Python, five years of LangChain, Kubernetes, AWS, vector databases, and prompt engineering.

Write:

> Own the operating path that turns customer-support requests into bounded agent actions. Integrate account data and approved tools, establish evaluation and escalation, control refund authority, measure total cost, and provide replayable evidence for every consequential action.

Technologies can follow. The outcome comes first.

## Separate durable capabilities from current implementations

A framework may be popular for eighteen months. The underlying functions remain.

Durable capabilities include:

- problem decomposition;
- distributed systems;
- interfaces;
- state management;
- data contracts;
- security;
- evaluation;
- optimization;
- observability;
- organizational communication;
- repair.

Current implementations include specific models, agent frameworks, vector stores, and cloud services.

A strong role specification identifies both and distinguishes required production familiarity from learnable tooling.

## Evaluate the manufacturing system

When a candidate uses agents heavily, the company should inspect how the work is produced.

Questions include:

- How is context admitted?
- How are tasks decomposed?
- What is delegated?
- What remains human-owned?
- Which validators exist?
- How are failures classified?
- How is generated code or analysis reviewed?
- How is identity preserved across runs?
- What receipts exist?
- What happens when a tool is unavailable?

The objective is not to punish leverage. It is to determine whether the leverage is controlled.

## Hire for repair capacity

Demos show the happy path. Production standing depends on repair.

Ask the candidate to show:

- a failed run;
- the transition that failed;
- the evidence used to diagnose it;
- the narrowest repair;
- the permanent guard added;
- the rerun result;
- the remaining unknowns.

A candidate who can repair a system responsibly is more valuable than one who can generate a spectacular first attempt.

## Use a capability matrix

Score candidates across functions rather than keywords.

| Function | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Outcome framing | Can translate business pain into acceptance criteria |
| Agent architecture | Can separate model, tool, workflow, and authority layers |
| Construction | Can build a bounded working slice |
| Evaluation | Can define or implement meaningful validators |
| Economics | Can measure total system cost |
| Governance | Can encode access, approval, refusal, and evidence |
| Repair | Can diagnose and improve failed execution |
| Communication | Can explain the same system to executives and engineers |
| Learning | Can acquire unfamiliar implementation detail quickly |
| Standing | Accepts responsibility and marks boundaries precisely |

This matrix supports a wider range of backgrounds without lowering rigor.

## Do not outsource role definition to the candidate entirely

An emerging-role candidate should help refine the category, but the company must still own the organizational decision.

Leaders need to define:

- budget;
- reporting line;
- production access;
- risk appetite;
- decision rights;
- team interfaces;
- success horizon.

A brilliant candidate cannot compensate indefinitely for missing sponsorship.

## The company's proof obligation

Hiring is a two-sided evidence problem.

The candidate must prove capability. The company should prove readiness.

Evidence of readiness includes:

- an accountable sponsor;
- a real workflow;
- access to stakeholders and systems;
- willingness to change process;
- a fair evaluation method;
- authority consistent with expectations;
- a path from prototype to production.

Without these conditions, the hire may become an isolated innovation theater role.


## A candidate is a production system

Every professional arrives with a system, whether or not they describe it explicitly.

The system includes:

- domain knowledge;
- habits of observation;
- tools;
- models and agents;
- collaborators;
- evaluation methods;
- reusable assets;
- decision rules;
- authority behavior;
- evidence practices.

Hiring only from a skill list ignores how these components interact.

Two candidates may both list Python and LLMs. One produces fast but unverifiable output. Another constructs slower initial paths that become reusable, evaluated, and cheaper over time. The noun list is identical. The production systems are different.

## Define the outcome system first

Before writing requirements, map:

1. the outcome the role owns;
2. the current production process;
3. the expensive bottleneck;
4. the decisions required;
5. the systems and people involved;
6. the authority available;
7. the evidence required;
8. the expected evolution of the role.

Then derive capabilities.

This reverses the common practice of copying a historical requisition and adding “AI.”

## Hire for correspondence

The employer should seek correspondence among:

- the candidate’s claimed identity;
- the evidence;
- the target workflow;
- the available authority;
- the evaluation;
- the actual role.

A brilliant autonomous-systems researcher may not be the right person for customer-facing workflow transformation. A strong product engineer may outperform a deeper model specialist if the bottleneck is integration and adoption.

Hire the system that corresponds to the problem.

## Evaluate tool leverage explicitly

Companies should decide whether tool and agent use is:

- required;
- allowed;
- limited;
- prohibited for a stated reason.

Ambiguity produces theater. Candidates may hide normal tools, and interviewers may confuse unaided performance with job readiness.

For agentic roles, evaluate:

- how the candidate selects tools;
- how context is bounded;
- how outputs are verified;
- how authority is controlled;
- how repeated work is reused;
- how failures are exposed;
- how cost is measured.

The ability to use leverage responsibly is part of the skill.

## A system-based scorecard

| Dimension | Evidence question |
|---|---|
| Outcome ownership | Has the candidate changed a meaningful result? |
| Architecture | Can they define objects, boundaries, and transitions? |
| Construction leverage | Can they coordinate people, models, and tools? |
| Evaluation | Can they establish what works and what fails? |
| Reuse | Do systems improve with repeated cases? |
| Authority | Do they respect identity, permission, and consequence? |
| Communication | Can they teach technical and business audiences? |
| Learning | Do they repair and encode failure? |

The scorecard should be populated with observed evidence, not adjectives.

## Consider the candidate’s network

For some roles, distribution and relationships are production assets.

A technical educator with 21,000 relevant followers may accelerate recruiting, category education, customer discovery, partnership, and adoption. A candidate with trusted domain relationships may reduce access time.

Do not value network size mechanically. Ask how the network corresponds to the role and whether the candidate has used it responsibly.

## Avoid impossible bundles

A company may write one requisition asking for:

- frontier model research;
- production platform engineering;
- frontend product design;
- enterprise sales;
- security governance;
- transformation leadership.

This is not evidence of a unicorn role. It is evidence that the company has not decomposed the outcome system.

Separate mandatory ownership from partner capabilities. State which team members and platforms already exist.

## Compensation should reflect leverage and consequence

If one person is expected to coordinate work formerly distributed across several roles, compensation cannot be benchmarked only against the narrowest title.

Consider:

- economic impact;
- span of systems;
- authority;
- risk carried;
- reusable assets created;
- organizational leverage;
- market scarcity.

This does not mean paying for output volume alone. It means pricing the production system and standing.

## The hiring receipt

After hiring, record what evidence justified the decision and what outcome the role is expected to produce.

At six and twelve months, compare:

- predicted strengths;
- actual outcomes;
- ramp time;
- evaluation accuracy;
- missing support;
- role changes;
- evidence that the interview missed.

Hiring should be a learning system too.

## Field exercise: Rewrite one requisition

Take an existing job description.

Replace the first half with:

1. outcome;
2. current failure pattern;
3. system boundaries;
4. authority;
5. evidence standard;
6. first ninety-day receipt.

Then restore only the technologies that are genuinely necessary.

The resulting role will be easier to evaluate and harder to fill with superficial keyword matching.
