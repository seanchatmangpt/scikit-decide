# 18. The Interview Is Often the Wrong Test

A hiring process is a measurement system.

It samples candidate behavior and tries to predict job performance. Like every measurement system, it can produce false positives and false negatives.

The post-LLM economy increases measurement error because many interview loops were designed for a different unit of work.

A candidate may be evaluated on memorized syntax, unaided algorithm implementation, or rapid puzzle solving while the actual role requires architecture, tool orchestration, evaluation, repair, communication, and production judgment.

The conclusion is not that all technical interviews are useless. It is that the test must match the claimed outcome.

## Start with the acceptance question

Ask:

> What observed behavior would give the company sufficient evidence that this person can perform the role?

For a conventional implementation role, a coding exercise may be appropriate.

For an Agentic AI Architect, stronger evidence may include:

- decomposing an ambiguous business problem;
- defining an agent and tool topology;
- identifying authority boundaries;
- designing evaluation;
- estimating runtime economics;
- constructing a bounded prototype;
- diagnosing a failed run;
- explaining the architecture to executives and engineers;
- producing a receipt.

A test that does not sample these behaviors cannot establish the intended standing.

## The portfolio execution review

Instead of asking the candidate to narrate a project, inspect one artifact deeply.

Review:

- source identity;
- problem definition;
- constraints;
- architecture;
- candidate's contribution;
- model or tool use;
- tests;
- failures;
- repairs;
- observed execution;
- remaining uncertainty.

This is more difficult to fake than a polished story because the interviewer can traverse the evidence.

## The bounded architecture challenge

Give the candidate a realistic problem with incomplete information.

Example:

> A customer-support agent can read account data, issue refunds, and communicate with customers. Design the system that determines when the agent may act autonomously, when approval is required, how cost is controlled, and how every consequential action is replayed.

Evaluate:

- questions asked;
- assumptions marked;
- boundaries preserved;
- reversible options considered;
- evaluation plan;
- operational evidence;
- communication.

Do not reward unnecessary complexity.

## The agent-assisted work sample

Permit the tools the candidate will use on the job.

Observe:

- how they frame the task;
- how they inspect context;
- which work they delegate;
- how they validate generated output;
- how they react to failure;
- whether they preserve evidence;
- whether they can explain the final result.

The question is not whether the candidate can produce without tools. It is whether they can produce trustworthy outcomes with the authorized production system.

A company may still test fundamentals separately. But fundamentals should not substitute for the work sample.

## The replay interview

Give the candidate a failed agent trace, incident, or workflow receipt.

Ask them to:

1. identify the failed transition;
2. classify observed versus inferred facts;
3. propose the narrowest repair;
4. define a permanent guard;
5. state what must be rerun;
6. describe the remaining standing.

This tests reliability thinking rather than presentation polish.

## How a candidate can redirect the process

You may not control the interview format. You can still improve the signal.

Say:

> I understand why you use this screen. It measures direct implementation under time pressure. My differentiating capability is designing and operating agent-assisted systems with mechanical verification. After the screen, I would like to show a bounded example that demonstrates that work directly.

This preserves the existing fence while requesting a relevant extension.

If the screen is a known poor predictor of your work and the company refuses any alternative evidence, decide whether the opportunity is worth the cost. Not every process deserves repeated attempts.

## Avoid self-exemption

The claim that an interview is misaligned can become a way to avoid legitimate evaluation.

You still need to establish:

- technical understanding;
- ability to reason about failure;
- truthful contribution;
- communication;
- judgment;
- capacity to learn;
- observed execution.

A new test should be more rigorous, not easier.


## Every interview is a proxy

An employer cannot observe years of future work before hiring. It chooses proxies: credentials, conversation, whiteboard problems, take-home projects, references, portfolios, or prior titles.

The question is not whether an interview is artificial. It is whether the proxy corresponds to the target work closely enough to justify the decision.

Post-LLM roles expose weak correspondence because the work itself may depend on agents, tools, retrieval, experimentation, and system-level judgment. A test that prohibits those capabilities may measure a different profession.

## Why conventional screens persist

Coding and puzzle screens survive because they are:

- standardized;
- scalable;
- easy to compare;
- familiar to interviewers;
- defensible inside existing process;
- believed to reveal general ability.

Those advantages are real. The problem appears when process convenience outranks role validity.

A candidate should understand the institutional reason for the screen before challenging it. The objective is not to insult the test. It is to offer a lower-risk alternative.

## Separate baseline from differentiator

A high-leverage agent operator may still require baseline technical competence. The evaluation can test that without making unaided implementation the whole decision.

Use two components:

1. **Baseline assurance:** Can the candidate reason about code, architecture, data, security, and failure?
2. **Role differentiator:** Can the candidate construct and govern an agent-enabled outcome system?

Many processes test the first imperfectly and omit the second entirely.

## A better work sample

A valid agentic-architecture exercise might provide:

- a real business workflow;
- sample data;
- tool constraints;
- authority boundaries;
- cost limits;
- acceptance criteria;
- known failure cases.

The candidate may use normal tools and agents. They must return:

- a system design;
- a working bounded path;
- an evaluation report;
- typed failures;
- cost and risk analysis;
- a replayable demonstration;
- a statement of unsupported claims.

This measures manufacture, not memory theater.

## Portfolio execution review

For senior candidates, existing work may be more predictive than a synthetic assignment.

A portfolio execution review should ask the candidate to:

1. choose one consequential project;
2. identify the exact subject and revision;
3. replay a meaningful path;
4. show the evidence;
5. explain one failure and repair;
6. state what remains unsupported;
7. map the pattern to the employer’s environment.

This makes unusually high output inspectable rather than unbelievable.

## Negotiate the evaluation

A useful script is:

> “I understand why the company uses this screen: you need a consistent signal of technical competence. The role we are discussing also depends on architecture, agent orchestration, validation, and production evidence. I would like to complete a bounded work sample or portfolio execution review that demonstrates those capabilities directly. I am comfortable being evaluated rigorously; I want the test to correspond to the work.”

This is neither evasion nor entitlement. It is an architectural objection with an alternative.

## Know when to walk away

Some employers cannot evaluate or use your leverage.

Warning signs include:

- tools prohibited in the job despite being prohibited only in the interview;
- no one owns AI architecture;
- the company wants transformation without authority or budget;
- evidence is valued less than conformity;
- the role combines several senior disciplines at a junior level;
- the employer treats agent use as cheating while demanding agent outcomes;
- the evaluation cannot be discussed.

A failed process may be market evidence, not a verdict on your capability.

## Employers need evaluation receipts too

Companies should record why their hiring method is believed to predict success.

They can compare:

- interview scores;
- work-sample performance;
- on-the-job outcomes;
- retention;
- ramp time;
- performance by candidate background;
- false-negative evidence from rejected candidates.

A hiring process should learn. Otherwise it becomes tradition disguised as rigor.

## The candidate’s responsibility

Do not use criticism of interviews to avoid gaps.

If the role requires reading code, reasoning about systems, communicating under pressure, or understanding algorithms, develop those capabilities. Tool leverage does not excuse foundational confusion.

The correct position is:

> Evaluate the real capability completely—including the ability to use modern systems responsibly.

## Field exercise: Design your ideal evaluation

For your target role, write five required behaviors.

For each behavior, define:

- the interview activity;
- the evidence collected;
- the time required;
- the acceptable tool policy;
- the failure condition.

Package the result as a one-page evaluation proposal.

When a hiring team asks how to assess an unfamiliar candidate, you will have an answer.
