# 15. Build in Public Without Becoming a Content Machine

Building in public is often described as a publishing habit.

That framing leads people to manufacture posts instead of manufacturing knowledge.

The post-LLM professional needs a different loop:

```text
Observe
    ↓
Explain
    ↓
Build
    ↓
Verify
    ↓
Publish
    ↓
Learn
```

Publishing is one transition in the system. It is not the purpose of the system.

## Start with an observation stream

Create a bounded set of sources:

- industry releases;
- research papers;
- customer questions;
- job descriptions;
- recruiter conversations;
- production incidents;
- repositories;
- policy changes;
- community demonstrations.

The stream should support your positioning. Following everything creates noise.

For a post-LLM career campaign, useful categories may include:

- personal-agent ecosystems such as OpenClaw;
- MCP and A2A;
- agent reliability and security;
- inference economics;
- enterprise adoption;
- emerging roles;
- new hiring methods;
- evidence and evaluation.

## Add interpretation, not summary

A summary answers, "What happened?"

Interpretation answers:

- Why does this matter now?
- Which assumption changed?
- What does the example prove?
- What remains unproven?
- Who should act differently?
- What new role or capability becomes necessary?

Interpretation is the source of authority.

A feed can aggregate news. A model can summarize it. Your value is the coherent thesis that helps the audience understand the transition.

## Build before you generalize

Public ideas become more credible when they encounter implementation.

If you claim that semantic caching reduces repeated intelligence cost, build a bounded cache and measure it. If you claim that agents need receipted actuation, create one end-to-end path. If you claim that conventional interviews miss agent operators, design and test an alternative work sample.

The build does not need to become a product. It needs to expose reality.

Implementation reveals constraints that commentary misses.

## Publish evidence-bearing units

A strong public unit contains:

1. **Observation:** the event, pattern, or problem.
2. **Thesis:** the non-obvious interpretation.
3. **Evidence:** source, demonstration, or result.
4. **Boundary:** what the evidence does not establish.
5. **Implication:** what the audience should change.
6. **Receipt:** where deeper inspection is possible.

This structure resists the pressure to sound certain about everything.

## Use an editorial portfolio

Not every post should perform the same job.

### Category posts

Explain the market transition and attract broad attention.

### Practitioner posts

Teach methods, architectures, and failure modes.

### Proof posts

Show observed execution, benchmarks, and artifacts.

### Career posts

Translate the category into roles, hiring, and professional action.

### Personal posts

Explain the lived experience, motivation, failure, or conviction behind the work.

A balanced portfolio prevents your identity from collapsing into either pure self-promotion or anonymous analysis.

## Repost with standing

Reposting high-performing ecosystem content is legitimate when you add value and preserve attribution.

Use this pattern:

> **What happened:** one factual sentence.
>
> **Why it matters:** the market or architectural implication.
>
> **What not to conclude:** the boundary.
>
> **What I would ask next:** the next test or decision.

This makes you a curator with judgment.

## Protect the work from the feed

Feed metrics can distort the research agenda.

Create two backlogs:

- **Public backlog:** items likely to help the audience now.
- **Foundational backlog:** work necessary for the thesis even if it performs poorly.

Your deepest architecture may never become viral. It still matters because it supports the public claims and differentiates you in consequential conversations.

Use the feed for distribution, not truth.

## Connect publication to opportunity

Every substantial item should lead somewhere:

- a portfolio page;
- a chapter;
- a demonstration;
- a workshop;
- a role taxonomy;
- a recruiter guide;
- an executive briefing;
- a conversation.

The call to action can be light, but your availability should not be secret.

A useful closing line is:

> I am exploring principal and architecture roles where this transition must be turned into a production system.

It is specific enough to create routing without turning the article into an advertisement.


## Public work needs a governing thesis

Without a thesis, building in public becomes an activity feed.

You announce commits, screenshots, releases, tools, and ideas. The audience sees motion but cannot determine what the motion means.

A governing thesis compresses the activity into a market position.

For this book, the thesis might be:

> As generated intelligence becomes abundant, value moves toward outcome definition, agent-system architecture, validation, reusable policy, authority, and receipts.

A post about OpenClaw, a planning framework, a hiring failure, a cache, or a résumé can all reinforce that thesis.

## Publish complete units of learning

A complete public unit contains:

1. an observation;
2. a non-obvious interpretation;
3. evidence;
4. a boundary;
5. an implication;
6. a next question or action.

For example:

> **Observation:** An agent can now coordinate tools across several applications.
>
> **Interpretation:** The interface is shifting from navigation to delegated intent.
>
> **Evidence:** A bounded demonstration with named tools and observed output.
>
> **Boundary:** The demonstration does not establish production governance or economic superiority.
>
> **Implication:** Companies need architecture for authority, validation, and replay.
>
> **Next question:** Who owns the action when the agent crosses system boundaries?

This unit creates value even for a reader who never inspects your project.

## Use three publishing depths

### Signal post

A concise explanation attached to a current event, demonstration, or question. Its purpose is discovery.

### Teaching essay

A structured argument that gives the audience a reusable model. Its purpose is authority.

### Proof artifact

A repository, benchmark, architecture document, demonstration, or receipt. Its purpose is trust.

The depths should link to one another. A signal post can lead to an essay. The essay can lead to proof. The proof can generate the next signal.

## The 21,000-follower advantage

An established audience changes the economics of experimentation.

You can test:

- which role language people understand;
- which pain attracts executives;
- which explanation recruiters can repeat;
- which technical distinction practitioners challenge;
- which proof causes profile and repository inspection;
- which topics produce qualified private conversations.

The audience is not merely a broadcast list. It is a distributed sensing system.

Respect that system. Do not convert every interaction into a sales attempt. Return value through explanation, curation, attribution, and response.

## Create a content-to-proof map

For each major proof object, list ten lessons it can support.

A planning framework might support posts about:

- why agents need policies;
- stochastic versus deterministic work;
- simulation before execution;
- plan reuse;
- exception handling;
- cost-aware action selection;
- the difference between generation and decision;
- new agent-system roles;
- enterprise governance;
- career leverage.

For each recurring content theme, identify the proof that keeps it grounded.

This two-way mapping prevents both empty commentary and incomprehensible project announcements.

## Protect your signal from volume

Agents make it possible to produce more posts than your audience can meaningfully absorb.

Do not measure publishing capacity by drafts generated. Measure it by ideas worth associating with your name.

Use a publication gate:

- Is the claim true enough?
- Is the lesson distinct?
- Is the source credited?
- Is the evidence proportionate?
- Does the post serve the governing thesis?
- Is there a reason to publish now?
- Would I defend the statement in front of a qualified critic?

A smaller number of coherent posts can build a stronger category than daily noise.

## Public correction is an asset

If a claim is wrong or overstated, correct it visibly.

A useful correction states:

- what you claimed;
- what evidence changed;
- what remains true;
- what you learned;
- how the operating system will prevent repetition.

This behavior differentiates evidence-bounded authority from personal-brand performance.

## Convert engagement into relationship

Do not treat every commenter as a lead. Look for repeated, substantive interaction.

A relationship path may be:

```text
comment
    → thoughtful reply
    → follow or connection
    → exchange of evidence
    → private conversation
    → collaboration or referral
```

The transition should be earned by relevance.

## Build a canonical archive

LinkedIn is the distribution surface, not the permanent book of record.

Preserve your strongest work in:

- this mdBook;
- a newsletter archive;
- a personal site;
- repositories;
- downloadable briefs;
- a searchable evidence index.

The public feed is ephemeral. The archive allows your ideas to compound.

## Field exercise: Publish one complete unit

Choose one active category event.

Write the six components: observation, thesis, evidence, boundary, implication, receipt.

Then classify the post as category, practitioner, proof, career, or personal.

After publication, measure:

- relevant profile views;
- conversations with target roles;
- evidence clicks;
- questions received;
- new objections discovered.

Do not ask only whether the post performed.

Ask whether the career system learned.
