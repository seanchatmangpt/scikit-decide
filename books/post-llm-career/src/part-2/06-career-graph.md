# 6. Build Your Career Graph

A list of skills is a poor model of a career.

It treats Python, negotiation, healthcare, architecture, writing, and leadership as independent labels. In reality, value comes from relationships among capabilities:

- a domain in which you understand the consequences;
- a tool you can apply;
- an outcome you can produce;
- evidence that establishes reliability;
- authority you can carry;
- people who trust you;
- systems that make the capability reusable.

A graph preserves those relationships.

## The seven-node model

Your Post-LLM Career Graph contains seven primary node types.

### Outcomes

What changes can you reliably produce?

Examples:

- reduce deployment failure;
- increase qualified sales conversations;
- shorten claims processing;
- improve candidate conversion;
- migrate legacy interfaces;
- create a compliant operating workflow.

Outcomes should describe changes in the world, not activities.

### Domains

Where do you possess context that affects judgment?

Domains may include industries, technical areas, customer types, regulations, communities, and operational environments.

### Systems

What reusable machinery have you built or operated?

Examples include software platforms, playbooks, agent workflows, data pipelines, evaluation harnesses, templates, ontologies, and governance processes.

### Agents and tools

Which cognitive or operational capabilities can you delegate?

Include models, agent frameworks, protocols, developer tools, search systems, automation platforms, and deterministic software.

### Evidence

What can another person inspect?

Evidence includes metrics, repositories, demonstrations, reports, references, customer results, test suites, receipts, publications, and repeated public teaching.

### Authority

What are you trusted or permitted to decide and execute?

Authority may involve budget, production systems, customer commitments, hiring decisions, architecture, security, clinical actions, or organizational policy.

### Market

Who urgently values the combination?

A capability without an urgent beneficiary may be intellectually interesting but commercially weak.

## Edges create differentiation

The nodes themselves are rarely unique. Many people know Python. Many people have used LLMs. Many people have worked in finance.

Differentiation appears in the edges:

> Python **applied to** audited financial workflows **producing** faster close **verified by** reconciliation evidence **under** controller approval.

Or:

> Agent orchestration **combined with** enterprise architecture **producing** bounded automation **verified by** replayable receipts **for** regulated operations teams.

The graph explains why a set of common capabilities produces uncommon value.

## Build from receipts, not memory

Do not rely only on autobiographical recall. Careers are too large and memory is selective.

Collect source material:

- résumés;
- project documents;
- repositories;
- calendars;
- email threads;
- performance reviews;
- presentations;
- customer feedback;
- published articles;
- metrics;
- invoices;
- issue trackers;
- certificates;
- references.

Extract concrete entities and relationships. This is your observed graph.

Then mark confidence:

- **Observed:** directly supported by evidence.
- **Verified:** checked against an independent source or execution.
- **Inferred:** plausible but not directly established.
- **Unknown:** missing.

This prevents a common career failure: turning an impression into a claim.

## Query the graph for a role

Suppose you are considering an Agentic AI Architect role.

Query the graph for:

- outcomes involving AI systems or automation;
- domains involving enterprise delivery;
- systems involving orchestration, integration, or evaluation;
- evidence involving working software, architecture, or benchmarks;
- authority involving principal-level decisions;
- market nodes involving companies with stalled AI initiatives.

The result becomes the source for your résumé, profile, interview stories, and outreach.

For a frontend architecture role, run a different query. The graph does not change; the projection does.

## Find missing edges

The graph also diagnoses gaps.

You may have:

- strong tools but no outcome evidence;
- strong outcomes but no public explanation;
- strong architecture but no observed execution;
- strong domain knowledge but no reusable system;
- strong systems but no market urgency;
- strong proof but no authority story.

A missing edge is more actionable than a vague instruction to "learn more."

If you have no evidence edge, build a demonstration. If you have no market edge, interview potential beneficiaries. If you have no authority edge, clarify what decisions you actually owned.

## Build a canonical graph and public projections

Your canonical career graph should be maximal and private. It may include confidential projects, failures, sensitive relationships, and provisional ideas.

Public artifacts are projections:

- LinkedIn profile;
- résumé;
- portfolio;
- speaker bio;
- consulting page;
- interview packet.

Each projection must preserve truth while respecting audience, privacy, and space.

This model protects you from rewriting your identity every time you pursue a different opportunity. You maintain one source of truth and generate bounded views.


## Why a graph is better than an inventory

A list records possession. A graph records relationship.

Knowing Python, healthcare, sales, or project management is not valuable in isolation. Value appears when a capability connects to an outcome in a market through evidence and authority.

For example:

```text
Healthcare domain knowledge
    + workflow design
    + patient education
    + evaluation evidence
    + clinical approval boundary
        → safer discharge process
```

The graph reveals combinations that a keyword inventory misses.

## The seven node types

### Outcomes

Outcomes are changes in the world that another person values. “Write code” is an activity. “Reduce the time required to onboard a new customer without increasing support incidents” is an outcome.

Outcomes should be bounded enough to evaluate and broad enough to matter.

### Domains

Domains contain the concepts, constraints, failure modes, and institutional knowledge required to act responsibly. Domain knowledge is not just vocabulary. It includes knowing what counts as evidence and which mistakes are expensive.

### Systems

Systems are reusable arrangements that produce outcomes: workflows, applications, templates, ontologies, operating procedures, evaluation suites, agent networks, or decision models.

### Agents

Agents are delegated cognitive or operational capabilities. They may research, classify, draft, plan, test, monitor, or coordinate. Record what they can do and the evidence required before their output is trusted.

### Evidence

Evidence includes demonstrations, benchmarks, tests, customer results, references, artifacts, receipts, publications, and observed execution.

### Authority

Authority identifies what you may decide, approve, access, spend, publish, or execute. Many career descriptions omit it even though it determines the scale of consequence you can carry.

### Markets

Markets are not only industries. They are groups with urgent problems, budgets, and mechanisms for choosing help. A market node should identify the buyer and the pain, not merely the sector.

## Edges are where differentiation lives

Two professionals may share the same nodes but possess different edges.

One person knows a technology. Another has used it to solve a regulated workflow, can prove the result, and has authority to operate the resulting system. The edge structure creates the difference.

Useful edge labels include:

- produces;
- validates;
- governs;
- explains;
- integrates;
- optimizes;
- authorizes;
- teaches;
- sells;
- learns from;
- is trusted by.

The strongest positioning statements are compressed paths through the graph.

> I connect enterprise workflow pain to bounded agent systems, validate them through executable evidence, and teach technical and executive buyers how to deploy them responsibly.

## Find disconnected value

Career graphs often contain valuable nodes that are not connected publicly.

You may have:

- deep domain knowledge that does not appear in your technical portfolio;
- technical systems that are not translated into business outcomes;
- a large network that is not connected to a coherent thesis;
- impressive output without evidence of consequence;
- authority from prior roles that is absent from your résumé.

These are not necessarily capability gaps. They are graph-connectivity gaps.

The cheapest career improvement may be to create the missing edge: a case study, a post, a demonstration, an introduction, or a role brief.

## Graph debt

Graph debt accumulates when your public identity reflects an old configuration.

You may still be classified by:

- a former title;
- an early-career specialty;
- a technology that no longer represents your work;
- an employer’s category rather than your outcome capability;
- content that attracts the wrong audience.

Like technical debt, graph debt creates friction in every future change. Recruiters bring mismatched roles. Collaborators misunderstand your level. The platform recommends your work to people who cannot use it.

Repair graph debt deliberately. Update the headline, featured evidence, recurring topics, and role language until they project the current graph.

## Query the graph from the market backward

Most people construct a career graph from themselves outward: What do I know? What have I done?

Also query it from the market backward:

1. Which outcome is urgent?
2. Which buyer owns it?
3. What evidence would reduce the buyer’s risk?
4. Which systems could create the outcome?
5. Which domain knowledge is required?
6. Which authority must be present?
7. Which of those nodes and edges do I already possess?

This prevents your career strategy from becoming an autobiography in search of a customer.

## The graph is allowed to be plural

A person can hold several market-facing paths without becoming incoherent.

For example:

- Agentic AI Architect for enterprise workflows;
- frontend platform leader for AI-native products;
- public educator explaining the post-LLM labor market.

The paths remain coherent if they share a central graph: systems architecture, agent leverage, evidence, and public teaching.

Do not force your career into one noun. Require correspondence among the paths.

## Field exercise: Draw the first graph

Create seven columns or seven groups of cards: Outcomes, Domains, Systems, Agents and Tools, Evidence, Authority, Market.

Add at least five items to each group.

Then draw ten edges using verbs:

- produces;
- validates;
- applies to;
- depends on;
- is trusted by;
- is authorized for;
- reduces;
- teaches;
- reuses;
- serves.

Circle the densest cluster.

That cluster is your strongest current positioning.

Box the most valuable missing edge.

That edge should become your next project, proof artifact, or market conversation.
