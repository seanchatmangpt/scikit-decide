# 5. The Five Layers of Work

When people ask which work AI will replace, they usually look at the visible task. That is the bottom of the stack.

A more useful model separates work into five layers:

1. execution;
2. coordination;
3. judgment;
4. architecture;
5. standing.

Every profession contains some combination of these layers. The proportions change with seniority, context, regulation, and technology.

## Layer 1: Execution

Execution produces an immediate artifact or performs an immediate action.

Examples include:

- writing a paragraph;
- implementing a function;
- entering data;
- creating a presentation;
- drafting a contract clause;
- sending a standard response;
- calculating a forecast.

LLMs are strongest here when the task is well represented in training data, the output is easy to specify, and mistakes are inexpensive or detectable.

Execution is not unimportant. Nothing changes without it. But execution is increasingly available through many manufacturing routes: direct human labor, deterministic software, AI generation, agent workflows, outsourced services, and cached artifacts.

A career identity built only around execution becomes vulnerable when the manufacturing route changes.

## Layer 2: Coordination

Coordination aligns tasks, people, information, and time.

Examples include:

- sequencing project work;
- routing requests;
- scheduling meetings;
- collecting approvals;
- assigning tasks;
- tracking dependencies;
- escalating stalled work.

Agents can absorb large portions of coordination because it often consists of retrieval, comparison, messaging, and state tracking. But poor coordination systems can also accelerate confusion. The value moves from manually moving information to designing the flow and handling exceptions.

The post-LLM coordinator asks:

> What state transition should occur, what evidence permits it, and what happens when the transition fails?

## Layer 3: Judgment

Judgment selects among plausible alternatives under uncertainty.

Examples include:

- deciding which candidate to advance;
- choosing a treatment plan;
- prioritizing a product investment;
- accepting a security risk;
- interpreting ambiguous customer evidence;
- deciding whether a model output is adequate.

LLMs can provide candidate judgments. They can compare options, surface precedents, and simulate objections. But judgment involves more than ranking text. It depends on context, values, consequences, and authority.

The professional advantage is not refusing machine assistance. It is designing the evidence, criteria, and escalation boundaries around the decision.

## Layer 4: Architecture

Architecture determines how the system produces outcomes.

It defines:

- components;
- interfaces;
- information flows;
- authority boundaries;
- validation gates;
- failure modes;
- recovery paths;
- observability;
- reuse.

In an agent system, architecture decides which observations enter, which tools are available, which outputs are admissible, which actions require approval, and which evidence must be preserved.

Architecture is not limited to software. A recruiter can architect a hiring system. A teacher can architect a learning environment. A clinician can architect a care workflow. A salesperson can architect a discovery and follow-up system.

The post-LLM economy increases the value of architecture because abundant intelligence creates more possible paths. Someone must shape the possibility space.

## Layer 5: Standing

Standing is the legitimate capacity to bind an action to responsibility.

It answers:

- Who is allowed to decide?
- Who authorized access?
- Who accepts the risk?
- Who can explain the action later?
- Who repairs the consequence if the system fails?

A model may recommend a loan denial. It does not thereby possess the institutional standing to deny the loan. An agent may generate a deployment command. It does not automatically own production authority. A system may propose a medical intervention. It does not become the accountable clinician.

Standing is why "human in the loop" is often too vague. The question is not merely whether a person clicks approve. The question is whether the person has sufficient information, time, authority, and responsibility for the approval to be meaningful.

## Your layer profile

Different careers have different layer profiles.

A junior role may contain substantial execution and limited standing. A staff engineer may execute less code while shaping architecture and accepting technical risk. A manager may coordinate heavily but possess little authority over budget. A founder may hold standing across nearly every layer.

The goal is not to abandon execution. Direct work often provides necessary contact with reality. The goal is to understand where your value currently sits and where it should move.

## Move work deliberately

The transition can be managed layer by layer.

- Automate repetitive execution where evaluation is clear.
- Replace manual coordination with explicit state and event flows.
- Support judgment with evidence, simulation, and adversarial alternatives.
- document architecture so the system can be inspected and changed.
- preserve standing through explicit authority and receipt mechanisms.

This creates a career progression that is not dependent on job titles.

You can move upward within your current role by redesigning how outcomes are produced.


## The layers are not a prestige hierarchy

The five layers are a model of where value and responsibility sit in a production system. They are not a ranking of human worth.

Execution can require exceptional skill. Coordination can be the hardest part of a complex project. Judgment can fail without the evidence produced by frontline work. Architecture can become detached from reality. Standing without competence becomes dangerous authority.

A healthy system connects the layers.

The career problem appears when a professional is rewarded only for one layer while technology shifts the cost structure around it. A worker may remain excellent at execution while the market pays less for direct execution. A manager may coordinate a process that no longer needs the same number of handoffs. An architect may design systems without accepting evidence about their actual operation.

Use the layers to locate change, not to glorify distance from the work.

## Layer 1: Execution

Execution transforms a defined input into a defined output.

Examples include writing a report, implementing a component, reconciling an account, scheduling a shipment, preparing a filing, or answering a customer question.

LLMs and agents enter this layer most visibly because outputs can be demonstrated. But execution is rarely uniform. It may include:

- routine transformations;
- exception handling;
- physical action;
- tacit sensory judgment;
- relationship-sensitive communication;
- work performed under legal authority.

The correct question is not whether execution can be “done by AI.” It is which substeps can be constructed by machines, which require observation unavailable to the system, and which require authorized human action.

## Layer 2: Coordination

Coordination decides who or what should perform each part, in which sequence, with which dependencies.

Traditional coordination often manifests as meetings, tickets, status updates, reminders, and queue management. Agent systems can reduce this burden when states and transitions are explicit.

But coordination does not disappear. It becomes more architectural:

- defining workflow states;
- managing exceptions;
- allocating scarce resources;
- synchronizing across systems;
- resolving conflicting objectives;
- escalating when evidence is incomplete.

The coordinator of the future may spend less time asking for status and more time designing the system that makes status observable.

## Layer 3: Judgment

Judgment selects among alternatives under uncertainty.

Some judgments can be formalized as rules, probabilities, constraints, or optimization objectives. Others depend on values, political context, trust, or consequences that cannot be delegated merely because a model can recommend an answer.

To strengthen your career at this layer, make your judgment inspectable. Record:

- the alternatives considered;
- the evidence used;
- the assumptions;
- the risk tolerance;
- the decision rule;
- the result.

Judgment that cannot explain itself is difficult to distinguish from intuition or status. Judgment with receipts can become reusable organizational intelligence.

## Layer 4: Architecture

Architecture determines the objects, boundaries, interfaces, and allowed transformations of the system.

In career terms, architecture is not limited to software. A recruiter architects an evaluation process. A clinician architects a care pathway. A salesperson architects an account strategy. An operations leader architects a fulfillment workflow.

Architecture asks:

- What is the canonical object?
- Which transitions are allowed?
- What evidence admits a transition?
- Where can automation construct alternatives?
- Where is authorization required?
- What failure must be refused rather than hidden?

As generative capacity grows, architecture becomes the discipline that prevents abundant construction from becoming uncontrolled change.

## Layer 5: Standing

Standing is the recognized authority to make a consequential decision and the obligation to answer for it.

A model can generate a recommendation. A tool can execute a command. Neither fact alone establishes who had the right to decide, whether the action applied to the correct subject, or who owns the consequence.

Standing includes:

- identity;
- role;
- scope;
- authority;
- approval;
- consequence;
- replay evidence.

This layer is why “human in the loop” is too vague. Which human? At which transition? With what information? Possessing which authority? Recorded how?

A post-LLM professional becomes more valuable by making these questions operational.

## Layer migration

A strong career strategy moves portions of your work upward without losing contact with the lower layers.

For example, a software engineer may:

1. use an agent to construct candidate code;
2. coordinate multiple verification tools;
3. judge tradeoffs and failures;
4. design the architecture and authority boundaries;
5. accept responsibility for release readiness.

The engineer still needs execution literacy. But value is no longer limited to typing every line.

A marketer may:

1. generate campaign variations;
2. coordinate channels and experiments;
3. judge evidence and customer resonance;
4. design the learning system;
5. own budget and brand consequence.

The same migration can occur in almost every knowledge-work domain.

## The layer-balance test

For one important outcome, estimate the percentage of your time spent at each layer today. Then estimate where the market will reward time three years from now.

The gap is your development agenda.

Do not automatically aim for zero execution. Aim for the mix that gives you reality contact, leverage, and standing.

## Field exercise: Map a consequential outcome

Choose one outcome for which you are responsible.

Write one sentence for each layer:

- **Execution:** What artifacts or actions must be produced?
- **Coordination:** What must be sequenced, routed, or synchronized?
- **Judgment:** Which choices cannot be reduced to a fixed rule?
- **Architecture:** What system determines how the work happens?
- **Standing:** Who has legitimate authority and responsibility?

Now identify the weakest layer.

That weakness is likely constraining the entire outcome system.
