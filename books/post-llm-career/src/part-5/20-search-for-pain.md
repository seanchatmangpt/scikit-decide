# 20. Search for Pain, Not Titles

A title-first search asks:

> Which open requisitions match the name of the role I already know?

A pain-first search asks:

> Which organizations are experiencing a problem my capability graph can resolve?

The second search discovers hidden demand.

## Signs of post-LLM pain

Look for public evidence that a company is struggling with one of these transitions.

### Prototype to production

The company has demos and pilots but lacks reliability, integration, evaluation, or governance.

### Tool fragmentation

Teams use many models and agent frameworks without a coherent capability or authority architecture.

### Inference and review cost

AI increases output but also increases human review, model spending, or operational noise.

### Legacy migration pressure

The organization needs to transform large systems faster than conventional delivery permits.

### Agent security concerns

Tool access, data leakage, prompt injection, permissions, and auditability block deployment.

### Workforce redesign

Leaders know jobs are changing but lack a model for roles, evaluation, training, and accountability.

### Customer-experience discontinuity

The company needs interfaces that move from forms and navigation toward delegation and conversation.

### Knowledge fragmentation

Critical context is distributed across documents, systems, and people, preventing reliable agent operation.

These pains appear in earnings calls, executive posts, engineering blogs, job clusters, product announcements, conference talks, incidents, and reorganizations.

## Build a signal query

For each pain, create a set of signals.

Example: prototype-to-production pain.

- hiring for AI platform, agent, evaluation, security, and solutions roles at once;
- executive language about scaling AI;
- repeated hackathons or pilots;
- partnerships with model vendors;
- new governance committees;
- customer announcements without implementation detail;
- job descriptions requiring both architecture and hands-on delivery.

One signal is ambiguous. A cluster creates a hypothesis.

## Map the buying center

The person with the pain may not own the requisition.

Potential buyers include:

- CTO or CIO;
- Chief AI Officer;
- VP Engineering;
- Head of AI Platform;
- CISO;
- product executive;
- transformation leader;
- enterprise architecture leader;
- consulting partner;
- recruiter supporting these leaders.

Map:

- who experiences the pain;
- who owns budget;
- who can create or reshape a role;
- who evaluates technical evidence;
- who could champion the candidate;
- who can block the purchase.

Your outreach should teach the right member of the buying center.

## Use roles as evidence, not only destinations

A job description contains market observations.

Extract:

- stated problem;
- desired outcome;
- technologies;
- hidden constraints;
- contradictions;
- seniority signals;
- evaluation method;
- organizational home;
- missing capability.

Across twenty descriptions, patterns emerge. You may discover that companies use five titles for the same function or one title for five unrelated functions.

Use the corpus to refine your role taxonomy and public teaching.

## Score opportunity fit

A useful opportunity score includes:

- pain urgency;
- capability fit;
- evidence fit;
- authority fit;
- sponsor access;
- evaluation alignment;
- compensation fit;
- location and work-arrangement fit;
- organizational readiness;
- learning value.

Do not maximize application count. Maximize the expected value of a defensible conversation.

A role with imperfect keyword fit but strong pain and sponsor alignment may be better than a perfect title inside an organization that cannot use your leverage.

## Create a pain brief

For each target company, prepare one page.

### Observed signals

Only public or directly received evidence.

### Pain hypothesis

A bounded inference, clearly labeled.

### Relevant outcome

What you could help change.

### Proof

Which two or three receipts establish adjacent capability.

### Conversation question

A question that tests the hypothesis without assuming it is true.

Example:

> You appear to be expanding from isolated copilots toward tool-using agents. Where is the current bottleneck: integration, evaluation, authority, or adoption?

This question creates discovery rather than a generic request for work.


## Titles describe supply; pain reveals demand

A posted role is a company’s attempt to translate a problem into a purchasable unit of labor. The translation may be late, incomplete, or wrong.

Pain appears earlier.

A company may reveal demand through:

- an executive announcing an AI-first strategy;
- several disconnected AI job postings;
- a product launch with no operational integration;
- customer complaints about slow service;
- rising cloud or model costs;
- security restrictions that block experimentation;
- repeated reorganizations;
- acquisitions that create integration debt;
- public discussion of productivity pressure;
- employees describing manual workflows.

These signals can support a role before the requisition exists.

## Build a pain hypothesis

A pain hypothesis has five parts.

1. **Observed signal:** What did the company actually reveal?
2. **Operational interpretation:** What process or system may be failing?
3. **Economic consequence:** What might the failure cost?
4. **Capability gap:** What is likely missing?
5. **Falsifier:** What evidence would show that the hypothesis is wrong?

Example:

> The company has announced several AI assistants but continues hiring large manual operations teams. This may indicate that generation exists without workflow integration, evaluation, or authority. The consequence is duplicated operating cost and slow adoption. The missing capability may be agentic workflow architecture. The hypothesis is false if the assistants are already integrated and the hiring reflects unrelated growth.

A falsifier prevents confident storytelling from replacing research.

## Sources of public pain evidence

Use sources with different incentives:

- annual reports and earnings calls;
- executive interviews;
- product release notes;
- engineering blogs;
- job descriptions;
- customer reviews;
- support forums;
- security disclosures;
- conference talks;
- employee commentary;
- partner announcements;
- procurement documents where public.

No single source provides the whole operating truth. Triangulate.

## Rank the accounts

Score target companies on:

| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Urgency | Is the pain active now? |
| Consequence | Is the economic or strategic cost material? |
| Fit | Does your capability graph correspond? |
| Access | Can your network reach a buyer or bridge? |
| Evidence | Can you support the hypothesis? |
| Readiness | Can the company use the capability? |
| Authority | Is there a leader who can sponsor the role? |

Prioritize accounts with strong fit and readiness, not merely famous brands.

## Create pain-first content

A pain hypothesis can become a public lesson without naming the company.

For example:

> “Why companies with dozens of AI pilots still have no AI operating system.”

The post teaches the pattern. A private role brief can tailor it to the target account using public evidence.

This separation lets you create broad value while preserving the specificity required for outreach.

## The pain conversation

Do not open with a large solution claim.

A useful conversation sequence is:

1. “I noticed this signal.”
2. “In similar systems, it often indicates this transition failure.”
3. “Is that true here?”
4. “How is the company measuring the consequence?”
5. “Who owns the boundary between model experimentation and operational deployment?”
6. “Would a short architecture diagnostic be useful?”

The questions admit correction. They turn the hypothesis into discovery.

## Avoid opportunistic fear

Pain-first selling can become manipulative when it exaggerates risk or treats every announcement as failure.

Use boundaries:

- distinguish observed fact from inference;
- do not imply private knowledge;
- acknowledge alternative explanations;
- avoid weaponizing security or layoffs for attention;
- offer a constructive path;
- respect the organization’s existing competence.

The goal is to help the buyer recognize a solvable problem, not to create panic.

## Map pain to proof

For each target account, choose no more than three relevant proof objects.

If the company struggles with cost, show caching and reuse evidence. If it struggles with governance, show authority and receipt architecture. If it struggles with product adoption, show human-agent interface and workflow evidence.

A massive portfolio without selection transfers the work of relevance back to the buyer.

## Search for transition owners

The right contact may not have “AI” in the title.

Look for leaders who own:

- platform engineering;
- digital transformation;
- operations;
- customer experience;
- developer productivity;
- enterprise architecture;
- data platforms;
- security architecture;
- product incubation;
- strategic workforce planning.

The role should follow the outcome owner.

## Pain is dynamic state

Update the map weekly.

A new executive, budget cycle, product launch, incident, or hiring freeze can change both urgency and access. Record the date and source of every signal.

Your career campaign should operate on admitted, current observations rather than a static dream-company list.

## Field exercise: Build a ten-company pain map

Choose one pain category.

Identify ten companies showing at least two signals.

For each company, record:

- signals;
- pain hypothesis;
- likely buyer;
- relevant role family;
- strongest evidence object;
- one discovery question.

Rank the companies by urgency and access.

Your target list should be a model of need, not a collection of logos.
