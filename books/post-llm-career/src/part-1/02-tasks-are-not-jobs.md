# 2. Tasks Are Not Jobs

A job description often looks like a list of tasks:

- prepare reports;
- meet with customers;
- write software;
- review contracts;
- schedule resources;
- analyze data;
- manage projects.

This makes automation discussions deceptively simple. If a model can perform several items on the list, observers conclude that the job will disappear. If the model cannot perform every item, others conclude that the job is safe.

Both conclusions confuse tasks with jobs.

## A job is a bundle of relationships

A job includes tasks, but it also includes:

- access to systems and information;
- relationships with customers and colleagues;
- authority to approve or reject actions;
- responsibility for consequences;
- context accumulated over time;
- coordination across organizational boundaries;
- trust;
- exception handling;
- obligations created by law, policy, and culture.

The bundle matters because tasks can move independently.

A customer-success manager may delegate meeting summaries and account research while becoming more valuable at escalation, negotiation, and renewal strategy. A software engineer may delegate implementation and documentation while spending more time on architecture, evaluation, and production risk. A paralegal may automate document comparison while becoming more important in case organization and evidentiary quality.

The job changes shape before it disappears.

## Decompose without diminishing

Task decomposition is not an argument that people are collections of replaceable parts. It is a method for seeing where work can be redesigned.

For each task, examine six properties.

### 1. Frequency

How often does it occur?

Frequent work creates a stronger case for tooling, templates, caching, or automation.

### 2. Variability

How different is each instance?

Low-variability work may be deterministic. High-variability work may require retrieval, planning, or human judgment.

### 3. Consequence

What happens if the task is performed incorrectly?

A typo in an internal draft and an incorrect medication instruction are not the same automation problem.

### 4. Reversibility

Can the result be changed after execution?

Drafting is reversible. Sending money is not. Publishing can be partially reversible but reputationally consequential.

### 5. Evaluability

Can quality be checked mechanically or with a clear rubric?

Code may have tests. A schedule can be checked for conflicts. A strategic recommendation may require judgment and delayed outcome evidence.

### 6. Authority

Who is allowed to perform or approve the action?

A model's ability to generate a decision does not grant it the right to execute the decision.

These properties determine the appropriate manufacturing method.

## Four destinations for a task

A task can move to one of four destinations.

### Preserve

Keep it human because the activity itself creates value.

A difficult conversation may be inefficient but essential. A leader cannot automate every moment of trust formation and still expect the relationship to remain intact.

### Assist

Use AI to prepare, retrieve, compare, or draft while a person remains in the loop.

This is the most common near-term pattern.

### Automate

Convert the task into a bounded workflow with explicit inputs, gates, outputs, and exception paths.

Automation should reduce total work, not merely move work into review.

### Eliminate

Remove the task because it exists only to compensate for an obsolete process.

Many organizations automate unnecessary approvals, duplicate reporting, and repeated data entry instead of questioning why the work exists.

The eliminate option is strategically important. LLMs make it easy to accelerate waste.

## The job reconstruction exercise

After decomposition, reconstruct the role around outcomes.

Suppose a recruiting coordinator spends time on:

- copying candidate information;
- scheduling interviews;
- sending reminders;
- collecting feedback;
- explaining delays;
- resolving calendar conflicts;
- monitoring process health.

Several tasks can be automated. But the reconstructed role may become more important:

> Own candidate-flow reliability from application to decision, intervene when the process stalls, preserve candidate trust, and provide hiring leaders with evidence about bottlenecks.

The new role is not "the old role minus scheduling." It is an outcome owner with a different toolset.

## The danger of subtraction thinking

Subtraction thinking asks:

> Which parts of my job can AI take?

Construction thinking asks:

> Given the new capabilities, what more valuable system can I now operate?

The first question is defensive. The second creates options.

A writer can become an editorial-system designer. An analyst can become a decision-intelligence operator. A developer can become an agent-systems architect. A project manager can become a flow and constraint engineer. A salesperson can become a market-learning system owner.

Not every reconfiguration will produce a better job. Organizations may use automation only to cut cost. But the constructive question gives you a stronger position: you can show how the work should be redesigned rather than waiting for someone else to delete tasks from your role.

## What people continue to own

Human work does not survive merely because models are temporarily weak at a particular task. That is a fragile defense.

A more durable boundary is standing.

People and institutions own:

- the definition of acceptable outcomes;
- the authority to grant access;
- the decision to accept risk;
- the interpretation of contested values;
- the obligation to explain consequences;
- the responsibility to repair harm.

These functions may be supported by models. They should not become ambient model privileges.

The post-LLM professional becomes valuable by connecting abundant candidate intelligence to legitimate action.


## A job is a negotiated bundle

A job description makes a role look like a natural object. It is not. It is a temporary agreement about which tasks, decisions, relationships, risks, and outcomes one organization assigns to one position.

Two people with the same title may perform very different bundles. A product manager in a startup may conduct research, write requirements, analyze data, sell internally, and coordinate delivery. A product manager in a large enterprise may spend most of the week aligning stakeholders and navigating approvals. The title hides the production system.

LLMs make this hidden structure visible because they can enter the bundle at different points. They may draft one artifact, automate one transition, or reshape the entire handoff pattern. Therefore the correct unit of analysis is not the title. It is the task graph.

## Build the task graph

Start with a recurring outcome, not a list of duties.

For example:

> Produce a weekly customer-risk review that causes the right accounts to receive intervention.

Then map the work:

```text
Collect signals
    → normalize account data
    → identify anomalies
    → gather qualitative context
    → classify risk
    → propose interventions
    → obtain approval
    → contact account owner
    → execute intervention
    → measure result
```

For each node, record:

- the input;
- the output;
- the actor;
- the tool;
- the decision rule;
- the failure mode;
- the evidence retained;
- the authority required.

This graph reveals that “customer success manager” is not one type of work. It is a chain containing collection, interpretation, judgment, coordination, communication, and authorized action.

## Four task destinies

A task exposed to LLMs does not have only two possible futures—human or automated. It can move into one of four destinies.

### Eliminate

The task exists because an older process created unnecessary friction. For example, manually reformatting the same information for several systems may disappear when the systems share a canonical representation.

### Reduce

The task remains but requires less human time. Drafting, classification, or retrieval may become a small review step.

### Raise

The task becomes more sophisticated because the worker can now examine more evidence or alternatives. A financial analyst may move from assembling a model to testing more scenarios and explaining the decision.

### Create

New tasks appear: evaluation design, agent configuration, exception handling, evidence curation, policy maintenance, and system governance.

This is an ERRC lens for careers: eliminate, reduce, raise, create.

## Task exposure versus outcome ownership

A person can have high task exposure and low career exposure if they own a valuable outcome and can redesign the production system.

A person can also have low apparent task exposure but high career exposure if the market no longer values the outcome enough to support the role.

Consider a copywriter. Drafting text is highly exposed. But a copywriter who owns conversion performance, customer language research, experiment design, brand voice, and regulatory review may redesign the role around a broader outcome.

Now consider a specialist who performs a difficult but infrequently needed manual process. The task may be resistant to automation, but demand may still fall if the surrounding product or workflow disappears.

Therefore ask two separate questions:

1. Which tasks can change?
2. Does the outcome remain valuable, and can I own more of it?

## The handoff tax

Jobs are often organized around handoffs rather than natural capability boundaries. One person researches, another writes, another reviews, another approves, and another executes.

Each handoff adds:

- waiting time;
- context loss;
- translation error;
- status work;
- queueing;
- incentive misalignment.

Agent systems can reduce some handoffs by allowing one accountable operator to construct several artifacts through specialized tools. That does not mean one person should hold unlimited authority. It means the boundary between construction and approval can be redesigned.

The career opportunity is often not “automate my task.” It is “remove the handoff tax around an outcome while preserving necessary separation of authority.”

## Become the rebundler

The most defensible response to task automation is not to protect each existing task. It is to become capable of rebundling work.

A rebundler can:

- identify the real outcome;
- distinguish essential controls from historical habits;
- assign machine-suitable work to agents;
- preserve human judgment where context or consequence demands it;
- connect the stages with explicit evidence;
- redesign the role around the resulting system.

This is an architectural skill even when no code is involved.

Employers may not advertise for a “work rebundler.” They advertise symptoms: slow launches, inconsistent quality, duplicated effort, escalating AI costs, or pilots that never reach production. Your task graph gives you a way to show how the work could be reorganized.

## Field exercise: Unbundle your role

Create a table with one row for every recurring task in your current or most recent role.

For each row, score:

- frequency: low, medium, high;
- variability: low, medium, high;
- consequence: low, medium, high;
- reversibility: easy, bounded, difficult;
- evaluability: clear, partial, ambiguous;
- authority: self, manager, specialist, institution.

Then classify the task as preserve, assist, automate, or eliminate.

Finally, write a one-sentence reconstructed role that owns the outcome after the task bundle changes.

That sentence is often more useful than your current job title.
