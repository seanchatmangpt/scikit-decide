# 4. The Résumé Is a Lossy Compression Format

A résumé compresses a career into one or two pages.

Compression is necessary. Hiring teams cannot inspect every artifact, conversation, decision, and result from a candidate's history. But compression always discards information, and conventional résumés discard exactly the capabilities that matter most in the post-LLM economy.

They preserve employers, titles, dates, technologies, and bullet points.

They often lose:

- how a person learns unfamiliar domains;
- how they use agents and tools;
- the systems they have manufactured;
- the scale and speed of their output;
- how they validate work they did not write manually;
- how they handle authority and risk;
- the quality of their public reasoning;
- the reusable assets they created;
- the difference between activity and outcome.

The résumé is not useless. It is an insufficient projection of a richer capability graph.

## Why traditional bullets fail

Consider a common bullet:

> Developed scalable web applications using React, TypeScript, and Python.

This is easy to search, but it tells the reader very little.

- What changed for the customer?
- What scale was involved?
- What constraints mattered?
- Did the person design the system or implement a ticket?
- Was AI used, and if so, how was output validated?
- What remains reusable?
- What evidence exists?

Now consider:

> Manufactured and validated an agent-assisted migration system that converted legacy interfaces into tested React/TypeScript components, reducing manual implementation while preserving architectural constraints and producing replayable verification reports.

The second bullet describes an outcome system. It is still compressed, but it preserves more of the structure.

## Build the source graph first

Do not begin résumé writing with a blank document.

Begin with a source graph containing:

- outcomes;
- projects;
- domains;
- tools;
- roles;
- decisions;
- evidence;
- collaborators;
- constraints;
- authority;
- failures and repairs;
- reusable assets.

The résumé should be generated from that graph for a specific audience.

A frontend role emphasizes product surfaces, design systems, performance, accessibility, and React/TypeScript evidence. An agentic architecture role emphasizes orchestration, evaluation, protocols, governance, runtime economics, and systems proof. The underlying history remains the same. The projection changes.

This is not résumé tailoring as cosmetic keyword replacement. It is query-driven projection from a canonical career model.

## The evidence stack

A post-LLM candidate needs more than one document.

### Layer 1: Search projection

Your headline, title, location, skills, and role language help retrieval systems classify you.

### Layer 2: Executive narrative

A short summary explains the economic problem you solve and the leverage you bring.

### Layer 3: Outcome résumé

The résumé provides relevant chronology and evidence-bearing accomplishments.

### Layer 4: Proof portfolio

Repositories, demos, reports, diagrams, benchmarks, and case studies allow deeper inspection.

### Layer 5: Public teaching

Articles and posts demonstrate that you can understand and communicate the category.

### Layer 6: Live receipt

A bounded work sample, architecture exercise, or system demonstration shows observed execution against the employer's problem.

Each layer answers a different question. Trying to force all six into a résumé creates clutter.

## Throughput needs context

In the agent era, candidates may produce work at volumes that sound implausible under the old model.

A large number of commits, documents, tests, or generated artifacts can signal extraordinary leverage. It can also signal noise.

The correct response is not to hide throughput. It is to contextualize it.

Explain:

- the unit being counted;
- the time period;
- the systems used;
- the validation method;
- the proportion generated versus manually authored;
- the accepted failure boundaries;
- the outcome enabled;
- the artifacts a reviewer can inspect.

A count without a manufacturing model invites disbelief. A count with receipts becomes evidence of a different production system.

## The résumé should teach

For emerging roles, the hiring team may not understand what it needs.

Your résumé must therefore perform two functions:

1. establish your qualifications;
2. define the category in which those qualifications matter.

A strong summary might say:

> Agentic AI architect with 25+ years in software engineering and enterprise delivery. Builds bounded agent systems that connect LLMs to tools, planning, evaluation, and receipted execution. Converts repeated reasoning into reusable policies and reduces the distance from operational evidence to trustworthy action.

This gives the recruiter language to repeat. It also tells the hiring manager why the candidate does not fit a conventional "full-stack developer" box.

## Preserve the human story

A graph and a portfolio can become mechanically impressive while remaining emotionally illegible.

People still hire people. Your career story should explain:

- what you care about;
- what pattern connects your work;
- what you learned from failure;
- why the next role matters;
- what kind of responsibility you want;
- how others experience working with you.

The post-LLM résumé is not less human. It is more honest about the system around the human.


## Compression is unavoidable

A résumé cannot contain your whole career. The problem is not compression itself. The problem is compressing the wrong dimensions.

Traditional résumés overpreserve:

- chronology;
- employer names;
- conventional titles;
- technology nouns;
- assigned responsibilities.

They underpreserve:

- the operating problem;
- the system you designed;
- the leverage you created;
- the evidence that the result worked;
- the range of authority you held;
- the rate at which you learned unfamiliar domains;
- the reusable assets that survived the project.

In a stable labor market, chronology can act as a proxy for capability. In an emerging category, it often hides the signal.

## The résumé as an index

The post-LLM résumé should not attempt to be the complete proof. It should be an index into a proof graph.

A strong entry contains four layers:

1. **Outcome:** What changed?
2. **System:** What combination of people, agents, tools, and controls produced it?
3. **Evidence:** What can be inspected?
4. **Standing:** What responsibility or authority did you carry?

For example:

> Designed an agent-assisted migration factory that converted source schemas into tested transformation plans, retained human approval for production changes, and reduced repeated analysis through reusable mappings. Evidence: architecture brief, fixture suite, replay logs, and benchmark report.

The statement is still compressed. But it tells the reader where the value lives.

## Separate the machine and human surfaces

A modern résumé serves at least two readers.

The machine surface needs:

- recognizable titles;
- explicit skills;
- standard dates;
- unambiguous employers;
- plain formatting;
- role-specific language.

The human surface needs:

- a coherent thesis;
- consequential outcomes;
- proof;
- differentiation;
- evidence of judgment.

You should not force one surface to do all the work. Use a clean résumé for extraction and a portfolio or role brief for depth.

## Translate without flattening

Emerging work often uses language that a recruiter cannot safely forward. Your job is to translate it into established business consequences.

Instead of:

> Built PPDDL-to-POWL policy compilation with receipted actuation.

You might write:

> Built a planning layer that converts uncertain operating scenarios into executable policies, validates constraints before action, and preserves replay evidence for audit and improvement.

The technical detail remains available in the linked proof. The résumé carries the economic meaning.

Translation is not dilution. It is interface design.

## Metrics require a denominator

Post-LLM portfolios are full of dramatic numbers: tokens processed, commits generated, agents launched, code produced, time saved.

A metric without a denominator can mislead.

“Reduced cost by 80 percent” requires:

- compared with what baseline;
- for which workload;
- over what period;
- including which review and infrastructure costs;
- under which quality threshold.

“Created 3,000 commits” requires context about repository scope, verification, and meaningful outcomes.

Use metrics aggressively when they are defensible. Attach enough context that the reader can understand what the number means.

## The portfolio graph

Your proof should not be a folder of unrelated links. Build a graph.

A market thesis points to a role brief. The role brief points to two or three case studies. Each case study points to architecture, code, tests, demonstrations, and receipts. Public posts explain why the case matters. The résumé links to the highest-level entry points.

```text
Résumé
   ├── Role brief
   │      ├── Case study A
   │      └── Case study B
   ├── Public teaching
   └── Technical evidence
```

This lets each audience stop at the appropriate depth without losing correspondence to the same underlying work.

## A résumé cannot rescue a category error

Sometimes the market is evaluating you for the wrong role.

A high-leverage agent-system builder may be filtered through a conventional coding screen designed to test unaided recall. A strategic operator may be evaluated as a narrow implementation resource. A cross-domain architect may appear unfocused because the employer expects a linear specialization.

No amount of bullet polishing fully solves a category error.

The résumé must therefore do more than describe experience. It must teach the reader what role they are actually looking at.

A useful opening structure is:

> **Identity:** the role category.
>
> **Business consequence:** the problem you solve.
>
> **Operating method:** how you create the result.
>
> **Proof:** what establishes the claim.

This is the first act of teaching the employer how to hire you.

## Field exercise: Reconstruct one bullet

Choose one résumé bullet.

Expand it into the following source record:

- **Observed problem:** What was happening?
- **Admitted constraint:** What had to remain true?
- **Manufacturing method:** What system, process, or collaboration produced the result?
- **Artifact or action:** What changed?
- **Evidence:** How was the result verified?
- **Standing:** What responsibility did you carry?
- **Reuse:** What capability remained after the project?

Now compress the record into one outcome sentence and one evidence sentence.

That pair is the beginning of a post-LLM résumé.
